K-Means Clustering

Every model up to now was handed the answers: a y column of labels, and a score that said how often it got them right. K-Means is the first model on this course that has no answers at all. It gets a table of numbers and one instruction — split these rows into K groups so that each group is as tight as it can be — and it works the rest out by moving K points around until they stop moving. DECK 17 · 01 / 12 · The idea
Unsupervised learning
Nobody tells the algorithm what the groups are — not even how many
KMeans(n_clusters=K).fit_predict(X) → a cluster number per row no y anywhere

Every model so far in this course was supervised: you handed it X and y, it learned the mapping between them, and accuracy_score told you how well. Clustering removes the second column entirely. There is no y to fit, nothing to be right about, and no train_test_split — the algorithm is not predicting anything, it is describing the shape of the data.

Live Demo — the same points, with and without the answer key
🧠 The algorithm cannot tell you what a cluster means. It hands back 0, 1, 2 — nothing more. Whether cluster 2 is "young high-earners" or "customers who never answer the phone" is your job as the analyst, and you work it out by looking at the averages of each group afterwards (panel 10). The numbers themselves are arbitrary: re-run with a different seed and the same group may come back as cluster 0.

✓ Supervised — what you have done so far

  1. Input is X and y.
  2. Success is measurable: accuracy, R², MSE.
  3. train_test_split keeps you honest.
  4. Examples: KNN, decision tree, SVM, linear regression.

◆ Unsupervised — from today

  1. Input is X only.
  2. No ground truth, so no accuracy — you judge it by inertia and by whether the groups make sense.
  3. No split needed: nothing is being predicted.
  4. Examples: K-Means, hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN, PCA.
python — the whole model, in four lines
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=3, random_state=42, n_init=10)
labels = kmeans.fit_predict(X)      # note: no y

df['cluster'] = labels           # glue the answer back onto the frame
📌 Where the name comes from. K is how many groups you asked for — you choose it, the algorithm never does. Means is how each group's centre is computed: the plain arithmetic average of the rows currently in it. That is the entire algorithm in its own name.
DECK 17 · 02 / 12 · The idea
The five steps
Initialise, assign, update, reassign, repeat — until nothing moves

The whole algorithm is a loop of two moves that take turns. Assign holds the centres still and colours the points. Update holds the colours still and moves the centres. Neither move can ever make the clusters worse, so the loop always ends — usually in a handful of passes.

1 · InitialisePick K starting centres.
2 · AssignEach point joins its nearest centre.
3 · UpdateEach centre moves to the mean of its points.
4 · ReassignPoints closer to another centre switch over.
5 · RepeatUntil no point changes group.
Live Demo — press play and watch the centres crawl into place
K = 3
🔁 Why it always stops. Both moves lower the same quantity — the total squared distance from every point to its own centre (panel 05). Assign lowers it because a point only ever switches to a closer centre; update lowers it because the mean is the point with the smallest total squared distance to a set (panel 04). A number that only ever goes down, over a finite number of possible groupings, has to stop going down.
python — the loop sklearn runs for you
# 1. initialise: K starting centres
centers = X[np.random.choice(len(X), k, replace=False)]

for _ in range(max_iter):
    # 2. assign: every point to its nearest centre
    d      = ((X[:, None, :] - centers[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)
    labels = d.argmin(axis=1)

    # 3. update: every centre to the mean of its points
    new = np.array([X[labels == j].mean(axis=0) for j in range(k)])

    # 4 + 5. repeat until nothing moves
    if np.allclose(new, centers):
        break
    centers = new
⚙️ You never write that loop. It is here so the object stops being a black box — in practice KMeans(n_clusters=k).fit(X) runs exactly this, in C, with a smarter initialisation (init='k-means++', the default) and a cap of max_iter=300 passes. The break is what n_iter_ reports afterwards: how many passes it actually needed.
DECK 17 · 03 / 12 · The idea
Assignment · Euclidean distance
Nearest centre wins — and "nearest" carves the plane into regions

The assign step is the same measurement KNN used, applied to K centres instead of N neighbours. For a point \(x\) and a centre \(c\) with m features:

$$ d(x,\,c) \;=\; \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m} (x_i - c_i)^2} $$

Straight-line distance, Pythagoras in as many dimensions as you have columns. The point joins whichever centre gives the smallest \(d\). Note that the square root never changes which centre wins — so sklearn compares the squared distances and skips it.

Live Demo — move the mouse over the plot: the probe point measures all three centres
🗺 Those regions have a name. The boundary between two centres is the set of points equally far from both — a straight line, exactly halfway, at right angles to the line joining them. K centres therefore cut the plane into K straight-edged convex tiles, called a Voronoi diagram. This is also K-Means' biggest limitation: it can only ever produce blobs separated by straight cuts, so a ring around another ring, or two long crescents, are shapes it simply cannot find.
python — the assignment, spelled out
# one point, three centres
x       = np.array([4.0, 3.0])
centers = np.array([[2.0, 2.0], [5.0, 4.0], [1.0, 6.0]])

d = np.sqrt(((x - centers) ** 2).sum(axis=1))
d                       # -> [2.236, 1.414, 4.243]
d.argmin()             # -> 1   : it joins cluster 1

# after fitting, sklearn will do this for you for any new row:
kmeans.predict([[4.0, 3.0]])   # -> array([1])
📏 Distance is measured in the units of your columns. A salary column in the tens of thousands and an age column in the tens are not comparable, and the sum above will be almost entirely salary. That is panel 08, and it is the single most common way a K-Means result comes out meaningless.
DECK 17 · 04 / 12 · The idea
Update · the centroid is a mean
The best possible centre for a set of points is their plain average

Once the points are coloured, each centre forgets where it was and jumps to the average of the points now wearing its colour — column by column:

$$ c_j \;=\; \frac{1}{|C_j|}\sum_{x \in C_j} x $$

In a table with three columns, the new centre is simply (mean of column 1, mean of column 2, mean of column 3) over the rows in that cluster. That is what cluster_centers_ holds after fitting, and it is why a centroid is usually not one of your actual rows — it is a fictional average customer.

Live Demo — drag the centre anywhere; the squared-distance total is smallest exactly at the mean
x = 2.6
y = 7.2
🎯 This is not a coincidence, it is the definition. The mean is the unique point that minimises the sum of squared distances to a set of points — it is what "average" means once you square your errors. So the update step is not a heuristic that happens to work: given the current colouring, it is provably the best possible position for that centre. That is why the loop can only ever improve.
python — one update step by hand
X      = np.array([[1, 2], [2, 4], [3, 3], [8, 9], [9, 8]])
labels = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1, 1])

X[labels == 0].mean(axis=0)     # -> [2. , 3. ]
X[labels == 1].mean(axis=0)     # -> [8.5, 8.5]

# which is exactly what the fitted object stores:
kmeans.cluster_centers_
# array([[2. , 3. ],
#        [8.5, 8.5]])
⚠️ An average is not robust. One point far out in the corner drags its centroid towards itself and away from the crowd it is supposed to represent — the same weakness that makes StandardScaler fold under an outlier. If your clusters look sensible except for one that contains three rows and sits in empty space, you have found an outlier group, not a customer segment.
DECK 17 · 05 / 12 · Choosing K
WCSS · the number being minimised
Sum of squared distances — sklearn calls it inertia_

There is no accuracy here, so we need our own measure of "did that go well". K-Means uses the only thing it has: how far the points ended up from their own centre. Take every point, measure the distance to its centre, square it, and add all of them up.

$$ \text{WCSS} \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{K}\ \sum_{x \in C_j} \lVert x - c_j \rVert^{2} $$

The name is spelled three ways and they all mean this one sum: WCSS (within-cluster sum of squares), SSD (sum of squared distances), and in code, kmeans.inertia_. Small is good — small means every point sits close to its own centre, so the clusters are tight.

Live Demo — each distance drawn as the square it actually is, then added up
K = 3
² Why squared, and not just the distance? Squaring punishes stragglers. A point 4 units out contributes 16, while four points 1 unit out contribute 4 in total — so the sum cares far more about the one badly-placed point than about a little general looseness. That is deliberate: a cluster with one member stranded on the far side of the plot is a worse cluster than one that is merely a bit wide. Squaring is also what makes the mean the right centre (panel 04) — the two facts are the same fact.
python — inertia_ is not magic
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=3, random_state=42, n_init=10).fit(X)

kmeans.inertia_                       # -> 78.85144...

# the same number, computed by hand
d2 = ((X - kmeans.cluster_centers_[kmeans.labels_]) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
d2.sum()                             # -> 78.85144...
🚫 Never compare inertia across different datasets. It is a sum, so it grows with the number of rows, and it is in squared units of your columns, so re-scaling the data changes it completely. An inertia of 2,000,000 is not "worse" than one of 40 — they are answers to different questions. The only comparison it supports is the same data, the same scaling, different K. Which is exactly the next two panels.
DECK 17 · 06 / 12 · Choosing K
Increasing K always looks better
Which is exactly why you cannot pick K by minimising WCSS

Here is the trap. Add one more cluster and every point can only get closer to a centre, never further — so WCSS drops. Every single time. Push it all the way to K = n, one cluster per row, and every point is its own centre: distance zero, WCSS zero, a "perfect" model that has learned nothing except a list of your rows. That is textbook overfitting.

Live Demo — drag K up to the number of points and watch the score reach a meaningless zero
K = 3
!

WCSS is a measure of tightness, not of truth

A lower WCSS genuinely means tighter clusters. It does not mean a better description of the data, because the tightest possible description is always the one that memorises every row. Any metric that improves monotonically with model complexity cannot, on its own, choose the complexity.

So we do not ask "which K minimises WCSS" — the answer is always n. We ask "where does the improvement stop being worth it". That question has a real answer, and the elbow method is how you read it off.

🔗 You have met this shape before. Adding polynomial degrees in lesson 20 lowered training error every time, right up to the curve that threaded every training point and predicted nonsense in between. Adding tree depth did the same. Same story, same fix: watch the rate of improvement rather than the improvement.
DECK 17 · 07 / 12 · Choosing K
The elbow method
Plot WCSS against K and look for the bend

Fit the model once for every K you are willing to consider, keep inertia_ each time, and plot it. The curve falls steeply while you are still splitting genuinely different groups apart, then flattens once you have started cutting real clusters in half. The corner between the two — the elbow — is the K to use.

Live Demo — ten real fits, drawn as the loop runs; the bend is measured, not guessed
👀 Change the number of real blobs and the elbow moves with it. That is the whole claim of the method — the bend is not decoration, it sits at the K where you stop merging different things and start splitting one thing. When the data really does have separated groups, the corner is sharp. When it does not, the curve is a smooth slide with no corner at all, and the honest reading is "this data has no natural number of clusters".
python — the elbow cell from the notebook
# 1. an empty list for the WCSS values
wcss = []

# 2. fit KMeans once for k = 1 .. 10
for i in range(1, 11):
    kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=i, random_state=42, n_init=10)
    kmeans.fit(df_scaled)
    wcss.append(kmeans.inertia_)      # inertia_ is the WCSS

# 3. plot it
sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid")
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
plt.plot(range(1, 11), wcss, 'bx-', linewidth=2, markersize=8)

plt.title('The Elbow Method for Optimal K', fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')
plt.xlabel('Number of Clusters (K)', fontsize=12)
plt.ylabel('WCSS (Within-Cluster Sum of Squares)', fontsize=12)
plt.xticks(range(1, 11))
plt.show()
🫤 The bank data has no clean elbow — and that is a real result. The curve we plotted in class slid from 2.23 million down to 1.59 million with no obvious corner anywhere. With 40,000 rows and dozens of one-hot columns, customers do not fall into tidy separated balls; they fill the space. When that happens, K becomes a business decision — "how many segments can marketing actually run campaigns for?" — and the elbow plot is there to confirm that no K is being left obviously on the table.
🥈 The second opinion: silhouette score. When the elbow is ambiguous, silhouette_score(X, labels) scores each point on how much closer it is to its own cluster than to the next nearest one, and averages. It runs from −1 to 1, higher is better, and unlike WCSS it does not automatically improve with K — so it can be maximised directly. It is much slower on large data (it needs distances between points, not just to centres), which is why the elbow stays the everyday tool.
DECK 17 · 08 / 12 · Making it work
Scale before you cluster
K-Means is nothing but distance, so the biggest column decides everything

Suppose you cluster customers on age (18–70) and balance (0–50,000). Two customers 20 years apart differ by 20 in the sum; two customers £2,000 apart differ by 2,000. The age column contributes essentially nothing, and you have not clustered customers — you have cut the balance column into K slices and given it a fancy name.

Live Demo — the same eighteen customers, clustered raw and clustered scaled
🔪 Look at the raw result: the cluster boundaries are horizontal lines. Age is not being used at all — the split is purely by balance. Scale the two columns and the same algorithm suddenly finds groups that differ in both directions, which is what you were trying to discover in the first place.
python — the notebook's preparation, in order
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

df = pd.read_csv("bank-full.csv")

# 1. text columns -> 0/1 columns (K-Means only accepts numbers)
df_encoded = pd.get_dummies(df, drop_first=True)

# 2. put every column on the same footing
scaler    = StandardScaler()
features  = df_encoded.columns
df_scaled = pd.DataFrame(scaler.fit_transform(df_encoded), columns=features)

# 3. only now, cluster
k      = 5
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=k, random_state=42, n_init=10)
df['cluster'] = kmeans.fit_predict(df_scaled)
🧩 Two details in that cell worth pausing on. First, get_dummies comes before the scaler, because the scaler needs numbers too — and yes, the 0/1 dummy columns get standardised along with everything else, which is normal practice here. Second, fit_predict is fit and predict in one call, and it is written on the scaled frame while the answer is glued back onto the original frame — so that panel 10 can read the clusters in real units instead of z-scores.
⚖️ Which scaler? StandardScaler is the usual choice for K-Means, because the algorithm is built on squared distances and z-scores put every column's spread at 1. MinMaxScaler is fine too and keeps the 0/1 dummies exactly as they were — but it lets a single outlier squash every other value into a narrow band. Either way the rule is the same one from lesson 19: fit the scaler once, on the data you are clustering, and never on a per-column whim.
DECK 17 · 09 / 12 · Making it work
random_state & n_init
The loop always stops — but not always in the right place

The algorithm is guaranteed to converge. It is not guaranteed to converge to the best answer. Two centres that start inside the same blob will politely split that blob between them and never venture out, leaving another blob to be shared by a single centre. Nothing about that arrangement is unstable — no point wants to move — so the loop stops, satisfied, on a bad answer. That is a local minimum.

Live Demo — ten different starting seeds, each run to convergence, sorted by the inertia they reached
random_state = 1
🎲 n_init is the fix, and it is embarrassingly simple. Run the whole algorithm n_init times from different random starts, compute the inertia of each finished run, and keep the one with the lowest. Nothing clever, just refusing to trust a single throw of the dice. n_init=10 was the old default and is still what everyone types; since scikit-learn 1.4 the default is n_init='auto', which uses 1 with k-means++ initialisation and 10 otherwise.
n_clusters=8

K — how many groups. The only parameter that is genuinely your decision, and the reason panels 06 and 07 exist.

init='k-means++'

How the starting centres are picked. The default spreads them out on purpose — each new centre is drawn with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest existing one — which makes the bad start above much rarer. init='random' is the naive version.

n_init=10

How many independent runs to try before keeping the best by inertia. Costs linear time, buys a great deal of reliability.

random_state=42

Fixes the dice so the same data gives the same clusters tomorrow. Without it your cluster numbers — and sometimes the clusters themselves — change on every run, and nothing in your notebook reproduces.

max_iter=300

A safety cap on the assign/update loop. It almost never bites; convergence usually takes under twenty passes.

🔢 Even with a fixed seed, the cluster numbers mean nothing. Cluster 0 is whichever group happened to be found first. Change the seed, change n_init, add a row, and the same customers may come back as cluster 3. Never hard-code "cluster 2 = premium" anywhere — re-derive the meaning from the group averages each time you fit.

The model gives you numbers; you have to give them names

python — the profiling cells
cluster_sizes = df['cluster'].value_counts()
print(cluster_sizes)
print(round(cluster_sizes / len(df) * 100, 2))

numerical_cols = ['age', 'duration', 'campaign', 'pdays', 'previous',
                  'emp.var.rate', 'cons.price.idx', 'cons.conf.idx',
                  'euribor3m', 'nr.employed']

# the single most useful line in the whole notebook
cluster_means = df.groupby('cluster')[numerical_cols].mean()
print(cluster_means)
📊 And the picture that goes with it. sns.countplot(data=df, x='cluster') plus ax.bar_label(container) puts the exact count on top of every bar — the fastest way to spot a cluster that has swallowed 80% of your rows (K is too small) or one holding four rows (an outlier group, or K too large).
💡 The habit worth keeping. Fit, groupby('cluster').mean(), then write one sentence per cluster in plain language before you do anything else. If you cannot write the sentence, the clustering has not told you anything yet — and that is a signal to change K, change the columns you fed it, or accept that these customers do not come in groups.
DECK 17 · 11 / 12 · Making it work
Clustering the pixels of an image
A photo is a table: one row per pixel, three columns — R, G, B

This is the second notebook, and it is the demo that makes clustering click. Load a 400 × 600 photo and NumPy hands you an array of shape (400, 600, 3). Reshape it to (240000, 3) and you have an ordinary dataset: 240,000 rows, three columns. Nothing about K-Means knows or cares that the rows are pixels.

(400, 600, 3)  .reshape(-1, 3)  →  (240000, 3)

Cluster those rows with K = 8 and cluster_centers_ comes back as eight RGB triples: the eight colours that best summarise the whole photo. Repaint every pixel with the centre of its cluster and the image survives with a palette of eight. That is colour quantisation, and it is how GIF files work.

Live Demo — real K-Means over this canvas's own pixels, in RGB space
original · thousands of colours
K-Means · 4 colours
🎨 Where the compression comes from. Every pixel used to need three bytes for its own colour. Now it needs only an index into a palette of K — 3 bits at K = 8 — plus the palette itself, stored once. At K = 8 that is roughly an eight-fold shrink, and the picture is still perfectly recognisable. Drop to K = 2 and you get a black-and-white poster; go to K = 16 and most people cannot spot the difference from the original.
python — the exercise from the notebook
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from PIL import Image

image    = Image.open("dog_image.jpeg")
image_np = np.array(image)
print(image_np.shape)                 # -> (400, 600, 3)

# 1. every pixel becomes a row of three numbers
pixels = image_np.reshape(-1, 3)          # -> (240000, 3)

# 2. cluster the colours
k      = 4
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=k, random_state=42, n_init=10)
kmeans.fit(pixels)

print(kmeans.cluster_centers_.shape)   # -> (4, 3)      the palette
print(kmeans.labels_.shape)           # -> (240000,)   one index per pixel

# 3. repaint: every pixel takes the colour of its centre
new_pixels = kmeans.cluster_centers_[kmeans.labels_]
new_image  = new_pixels.reshape(image_np.shape).astype("uint8")

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 4))
plt.imshow(new_image)
plt.title(f"Image with K = {k} Colors")
plt.axis("off")
plt.show()
🧾 Three details that trip people up. reshape(-1, 3) lets NumPy work out the row count for you. cluster_centers_[labels_] is fancy indexing — it builds the full 240,000 × 3 array of repainted pixels in one expression, no loop. And .astype("uint8") is not optional: the centres are floats (a mean of integers rarely lands on one), and imshow needs whole numbers 0–255 or it renders a mess.
🐢 240,000 rows is enough to feel it. A full K-Means over every pixel takes real seconds. MiniBatchKMeans is the drop-in replacement for exactly this case — it updates the centres from small random batches instead of the whole array, and gets a near-identical palette in a fraction of the time.

07Where K-Means fails

Generate two concentric rings with sklearn.datasets.make_circles(n_samples=400, factor=0.4, noise=0.05), throw the labels away, and cluster with K = 2. Plot the result. Why does it cut the picture in half instead of finding the two rings?

hint

Every cluster K-Means can produce is a Voronoi tile — a convex region with straight edges (panel 03). A ring wrapped around another ring is not convex, so no pair of centroids can describe it. DBSCAN handles this data in one line.

The KMeans object
What it takes, what it stores, and what you can call afterwards

Everything with a trailing underscore appears only after fit — that is sklearn's convention for "this was learned from data".

attributeshapewhat it holds
labels_(n,)The cluster number of every training row. Same thing fit_predict returns.
cluster_centers_(K, m)The K centroids, one row each, in the units of the data you fitted on.
inertia_scalarThe WCSS of the best run. The number the elbow plot is made of.
n_iter_scalarHow many assign/update passes the winning run needed.
methodreturnswhen to use it
fit(X)the modelLearn the centroids. No y — pass one and it is politely ignored.
fit_predict(X)(n,) labelsFit, then hand back the labels. The one-liner you will use most.
predict(X_new)(rows,) labelsAssign new rows to the existing centroids, without moving them. This is the part that makes K-Means usable in production.
transform(X)(n, K)The distance from every row to every centroid. Occasionally useful as K new features.
📦 It is a normal sklearn estimator, so everything from lesson 20 still applies. Pipeline([('scaler', StandardScaler()), ('km', KMeans(n_clusters=5))]) is legal and is the right way to ship one, because it guarantees that tomorrow's rows are scaled with the same statistics before predict assigns them. joblib.dump saves the whole thing, centroids included.
python — fit once, then assign new customers forever
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
import joblib

model = Pipeline([
    ('scaler', StandardScaler()),
    ('km',     KMeans(n_clusters=5, random_state=42, n_init=10))
])
model.fit(df_encoded)

joblib.dump(model, 'segments.pkl')

# tomorrow, in another file, on a raw row:
model = joblib.load('segments.pkl')
model.predict(new_customers)     # scaled with the training stats, then assigned

# and to inspect the centroids in real units again:
km     = model.named_steps['km']
scaler = model.named_steps['scaler']
scaler.inverse_transform(km.cluster_centers_)
🔄 That last line is the one people miss. After scaling, cluster_centers_ is a table of z-scores — "0.8 standard deviations above average balance" is hard to present. scaler.inverse_transform puts them back into pounds and years, which is the form a cluster profile belongs in.
APPENDIX A2 · Reference
K-Means vs KNN
Same letter, same distance formula, opposite jobs

These two get confused constantly, and it is entirely the fault of the letter K — which means something different in each. In KNN, K is how many neighbours get a vote. In K-Means, K is how many groups exist. Everything else follows from that.

KNNK-Means
learningsupervisedunsupervised
needs yyesno
K meanshow many neighbours votehow many clusters to find
what fit doesstores the training rows — there is no trainingruns assign/update until the centroids settle
what predict doesfinds the K nearest stored rows, takes the majority labelfinds the 1 nearest centroid, returns its number
outputa class you already knew abouta group number nobody has named yet
scored byaccuracy_score against the truthinertia_, silhouette, and your judgement
choose K withcross-validation on accuracythe elbow on inertia
needs scalingyesyes
🤝 What they genuinely share. Both are pure Euclidean distance, so both are ruined by an unscaled column and both degrade in very high dimensions, where all distances start to look alike. If you understood why KNN needed MinMaxScaler, you already understand why K-Means needs StandardScaler — it is the same lesson arriving twice.
🧭 And the family K-Means belongs to. KMeans assumes round, similarly-sized, straight-edged blobs, and needs K up front. DBSCAN finds clusters of any shape by following density, works out the number itself, and labels the leftovers as noise — but needs a distance threshold instead. AgglomerativeClustering merges the two nearest groups over and over and gives you a dendrogram, so you can pick K after looking at it. K-Means stays the default because it is by far the fastest of the three on large data.
