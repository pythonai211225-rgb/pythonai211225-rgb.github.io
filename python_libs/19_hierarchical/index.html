Hierarchical Clustering

Above: eleven points merging one pair at a time, with the dendrogram building alongside them. The two long bars near the top are the four real groups holding out against each other — that is where you would cut.

K-Means makes you name K before it will start, and if you guess wrong you run it again. Hierarchical clustering refuses to ask. Every point begins as a cluster of its own, the two nearest clusters merge, and that repeats until everything is one — leaving behind a tree that contains every possible number of clusters at once. You pick the number afterwards, by choosing a height and cutting. The interesting question turns out not to be "how far apart are two points" but "how far apart are two whole clusters" — and there are five different answers.

DECK 19 · 01 / 08 · The idea
No K required
Every point is a cluster, until it is not

Hierarchical clustering is unsupervised learning that splits the data into groups by hierarchy. Every data point starts as a cluster of its own. Then, step by step, the algorithm merges the nearest clusters together — two into one, again and again, until a single cluster holds everything.

🌳 The payoff. You do not have to decide the number of clusters in advance. The algorithm builds the entire tree of merges, and only afterwards do you choose where to cut it. One run gives you every possible number of clusters — 2, 3, 17, all of them, already computed.

Because it merges upward from the bottom, this version is called agglomerative — from agglomerate, to clump together. (The reverse, starting from one big cluster and splitting downwards, is divisive, and is far less common because it is far more expensive.)

1
Start. N points means N clusters. 150 flowers = 150 clusters.
2
Find the closest pair of clusters, by whichever rule you chose.
3
Merge them into one, and record the height at which they merged.
4
Repeat until one cluster is left. The record of merges is the tree.
📌 Step 2 hides the entire subject. Two points have one obvious distance between them. Two clusters of many points each do not — and the choice you make there is the choice that decides your answer. That is panel 04.
DECK 19 · 02 / 08 · The idea
The dendrogram
A tree that remembers every merge

The most accepted way to draw a hierarchical clustering is a dendrogram — a tree that shows when and how points joined into clusters. On the x-axis are all the points; on the y-axis are the distances at which they merged.

📏 The one rule for reading it: the higher the bar that joins two points, the further apart they are.
An agglomerative dendrogram of 6 data points. N1 and N2 merged low down, so they are close. The final merge happens right at the top, so those two groups are far apart.

You can slice the dendrogram at any height in order to fix the number of clusters. The dashed line above crosses three vertical lines, so it produces 3 clusters instead of the 2 you would get by cutting higher up.

✂️ Where to cut. If clusters connect with a tall jump — a long vertical bar with nothing merging along it — that is a sign it is worth stopping and cutting just below it. A tall bar means those two groups held out a long time before merging, which is exactly what "these are genuinely different groups" looks like on a tree.
DECK 19 · 03 / 08 · The idea
Distance calculation
Pythagoras, one more time

In the first stage the algorithm computes the distances between every point and every other point. The most common distance is Euclidean distance, which is based on Pythagoras' theorem:

$$D = \sqrt{(x_1 - y_1)^2 + (x_2 - y_2)^2 + \dots + (x_n - y_n)^2}$$

For two points \(p\) and \(q\) with two features that is just the hypotenuse of a right triangle:

$$d(p,q)^2 = (q_1 - p_1)^2 + (q_2 - p_2)^2$$
📌 The smaller the distance, the more similar the points are. One extra feature is simply one more ( )² inside the square root — the formula does not change shape as the dimensions grow.
⚠️ The scaling trap, again. Because every gap is squared, a column running 20–250 contributes numbers in the thousands while a column running 6–13 contributes single digits. The big column silently decides every merge. MinMaxScaler or StandardScaler before linkage is not optional — and Ward is the most sensitive of all the linkages to skipping it.
DECK 19 · 04 / 08 · The five linkages
One example, five answers
How far apart are two whole clusters?

When we merge clusters and not just individual points, it matters a great deal how we measure the distance between them. There are several approaches, and each one gives a different result — so the right choice depends on the structure of your data.

Every method below is applied to the same two clusters, so the five answers can be compared directly:

$$A = \{(1,1),\ (2,1)\} \qquad B = \{(5,5),\ (6,6)\}$$
The same two clusters, three different questions — and three different distances.
Single Linkage — 5.0

Looks only at the shortest distance between two points, one from each cluster. It means most of the points can be far apart, but two points alone are close — and that is enough to unite the clusters.

Elongated or scattered clusters

Sometimes clusters are not round but stretched along a direction. Single Linkage can absorb that, because it does not demand the whole cluster be dense.

Chain-like connections

If two clusters have a single point between them acting like a bridge, Single Linkage catches it immediately.

Merging on a weak link

You do not care whether most of it is close — one close point is enough. It says: "if somebody knows somebody, connect the clusters."

⚠️ Weakness: it can create long straggly chains — clusters held together only by an indirect, single-point connection.
Complete Linkage — 7.07

The opposite of Single. It looks at the longest distance between every pair of points, one from each cluster. The clusters will only unite if all of their points are close to one another.

Tight, homogeneous clusters

You want to guarantee that no point inside a cluster is too far from any other.

Keeping strangers out

Even if one point is distant, Complete Linkage delays the merge until the clusters really are close as a whole.

Small, well-defined groups

Customers with very similar behaviour, with no exceptions allowed.

⚠️ Weakness: very sensitive to extreme points — a single outlier inflates the maximum distance between the clusters.
Average Linkage — 6.0

Computes all the possible distances between the points of the two clusters and takes the mean. A balanced method, sitting between Single and Complete.

A compromise

It does not demand every point be close the way Complete does, but neither is it satisfied with just the nearest pair the way Single is.

Noisy or scattered data

The average reduces the influence of one extreme point, so the result is more stable.

Natural clusters

Suited to geometric data, or behaviour with a general similarity rather than an extreme link.

⚠️ Weakness: less effective on data with clusters that differ greatly in size or structure.
Centroid Linkage — 5.7

Looks only at the centre of each cluster. It averages all the points in each cluster to get a centroid, then measures the single distance between the two centres.

the two centroids
centroid of A = (1.5, 1)
centroid of B = (5.5, 5.5)
distance between them  ≈  5.7
Similar to K-Means

It ignores the details and looks only at the general position of each cluster.

Symmetric, noise-free clusters

The centre represents the cluster well when the cluster is compact and not scattered.

Fast and simple

The easiest method to implement mathematically — all you need is averages.

⚠️ Weakness: it can unite clusters that are very far from each other, if their centroids happen to be close.
🔍 Centroid vs Average. Both use the word "average", but they compute completely different things. Average averages the distances between pairs of points; Centroid measures one distance between two averaged points. The difference has a big effect on the result.
Ward Linkage — not a distance at all

Ward is the odd one out. Although it is a method for computing the distance between clusters, it is built on a different approach from all the rest. It does not ask "how far apart are these?" — it asks "which merge would increase the variance inside the clusters the least?" That gets its own panel.

DECK 19 · 05 / 08 · The five linkages
Ward, step by step
Minimum rise in variance

At every merging stage Ward chooses the two clusters whose union would cause the smallest rise in variance — the internal variance. This creates clusters that are as uniform and tightly packed as possible.

1
Start — every point is a cluster of its own. If there are N observations you start with N clusters. For example: 150 flowers = 150 starting clusters.
2
Compute the internal variance of each cluster. At the beginning every cluster holds one point, so its variance is 0. As soon as clusters start merging, each one's variance has to be computed.
3
Check every possible pair. For each pair, ask: "if we merge A and B, by how much would the variance of the new cluster grow?"
4
Choose the pair with the smallest rise. Out of all the possibilities, take the one that produces the smallest increase in variance. This is the essence of Ward — preserving maximum internal uniformity.
5
Merge them. The new cluster is built, A and B are replaced by their union, and the list of active clusters is updated.
6
Repeat. Steps 2–5 run again and again until all the clusters are united into one. In parallel the dendrogram is built, documenting the whole process.
✂️ Determining the number of clusters. Nowhere in that process does Ward decide how many clusters there are — that depends on you. At the end you choose a cutting height. The lower you cut, the more clusters; the higher you cut, the fewer and bigger they are.
When is it good?
Clear, round, tightly-packed clusters

Ward looks for "statistical" divisions — ones that maintain the uniformity of the clusters.

A mathematical rather than geometric approach

It is not only a question of distance, but of how much "noise" the merge adds.

Numeric data, K-Means-like results

K-Means also minimises variance, so Ward is similar to it in behaviour.

⚠️ Weakness: it is liable to be sensitive to normalisation, so it is important to scale the data before use. If two clusters have very different variances, joining them will raise the variance — so Ward will avoid merging groups that are similar in average but different in spread.

Which one, and when
A summary, and where each is used for real
MethodWhat it measuresWhen it is suitable
Single LinkageThe shortest distance between two pointsElongated clusters, or soft connections
Complete LinkageThe longest distance between two pointsTight, homogeneous clusters
Average LinkageThe mean of every pair distanceNoisy data, when you want balance
Centroid LinkageThe distance between the two centroidsClear, clean geometric structure
Ward LinkageThe rise in variance caused by mergingVery uniform, densely packed clusters
📌 Note: every method builds a different dendrogram. The right choice depends on whether you want clusters that are tight, flexible, or balanced between the two. It is worth trying several and seeing which one represents the true structure of the data.
Where each one earns its keep
MethodReal examples
SingleFacebook / social networks: identifying giant communities (a friend brings a friend). Computer and internet networks: finding a minimal, optimal cable route between servers. Epidemiology: tracing a chain of viral infection between people.
CompleteRouters and Wi-Fi: assigning devices to routers so all of them are within close reception range. Assembling teams: building small working groups where everybody has to suit everybody.
AverageLinguistics and language families: classifying languages by overall word similarity. Medical diagnosis: grouping patients for treatment by a combination of measurements (pulse, blood pressure, sugar).
CentroidLogistics and shipping: merging distribution centres by geographic centre of gravity. GPS and navigation: grouping points of interest by city centre or neighbourhood.
WardNetflix / Spotify: segmenting customers into clear, balanced taste groups (rock fans, pop fans). Real estate: classifying neighbourhoods by socio-economic level and building style.
DECK 19 · 07 / 08 · In code
150 irises
linkage, dendrogram, fcluster

Note the imports: AgglomerativeClustering comes from scikit-learn, but the tree tools — linkage, dendrogram and fcluster — come from scipy. sklearn can cluster, but it cannot draw you the tree.

python
import pandas as pd
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.preprocessing import MinMaxScaler
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram, fcluster

# 1. read csv
df = pd.read_csv("iris_unlabaled.csv")
python
# 2. choose numeric columns
df_numeric = df.select_dtypes(include=['float64', 'int64'])

# 3. MinMaxScaler
scaler = MinMaxScaler()
df_scaled = pd.DataFrame(scaler.fit_transform(df_numeric), columns=df_numeric.columns)

# 4. build the tree
linkage_matrix = linkage(df_scaled, method='average')

# 5. draw
plt.figure(figsize=(12, 6))
dendrogram(linkage_matrix)
plt.title("Dendrogram (average Linkage)")
plt.xlabel("Data Point Index")
plt.ylabel("Distance")
plt.show()

# 6. cut the tree into clusters
cluster_labels = fcluster(linkage_matrix, t=6, criterion='maxclust')

# 7. attach labels back to the dataframe
df['cluster'] = cluster_labels

print(df['cluster'].value_counts().sort_index())

LineWhat it does
select_dtypesKeeps only the numeric columns — clustering cannot measure a distance to a piece of text.
MinMaxScalerPuts every column on the same 0–1 ruler, so no one measurement dominates the distance.
linkage(df, method=)Does all the merging and returns the linkage matrix — the recipe for the whole tree.
dendrogram(...)Draws that matrix as the tree.
fcluster(t=6, 'maxclust')Cuts the tree so 6 clusters come out, returning a label 1..6 for every row.
🔍 Read those counts. Clusters 1, 4 and 5 hold 49, 46 and 49 flowers — three healthy groups. But clusters 2 and 6 hold one flower each, and cluster 3 holds four. Those tiny clusters are the give-away of average linkage on data with a few odd points: rather than absorb them, the tree left them dangling on their own branch until the very end. Cut at t=3, or switch to method='ward', and the three balanced groups the iris data actually has come straight out.
💡 criterion='maxclust' means "give me exactly this many clusters". The alternative, criterion='distance', means "cut at this height and give me however many that produces" — which is the version that matches the dashed line in panel 02.

🌳 The honest summary. Hierarchical clustering buys you two things K-Means cannot give: you do not have to guess K, and you get the same answer every single run. It charges you for both — it has to compute the distance between every pair of points, so on tens of thousands of rows it becomes impractical while K-Means keeps going.
📊 A common working pattern: run hierarchical clustering on a sample of a large dataset, read the dendrogram to decide how many clusters the data really has, then run K-Means with that K on the full table.
