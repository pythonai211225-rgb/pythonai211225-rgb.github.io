Principal Component Analysis

K-Means took a table and grouped its rows. PCA takes the same table and shrinks its columns — thirty of them down to two — without simply deleting twenty-eight. It builds a handful of new columns, each one a weighted blend of all the old ones, chosen so the rows stay as spread out as possible. And once you have those weights, using PCA is one multiplication: a row of thirty numbers times a component of thirty weights gives one number. Watch a cloud of two columns become a line of one:

DECK 18 · 01 / 10 · The idea
Too many columns
Thirty features is not thirty times as informative as one

The tumour table in the notebook has 569 rows and 30 columns. Every column is a real measurement of a cell nucleus, and none of them is obviously junk. But thirty columns brings three problems at once, and PCA is aimed at all three.

You cannot see it

A scatter plot has two axes. With 30 columns there are 435 possible pairs, and none of them shows you the whole shape. Two components can.

It is slow

Distance-based models — KNN, K-Means, SVM — do work proportional to the number of columns, on every row, every time.

It overfits

More columns means more ways for a model to memorise noise. Fewer, denser columns generalise better.

There is a fourth reason, and it is the one that makes PCA work at all: the columns repeat each other. In that tumour table, mean radius, mean perimeter and mean area are three measurements of the same underlying thing — how big the nucleus is. They rise and fall together. Three columns, roughly one fact. PCA finds those repetitions and folds them into a single new column.

🎯 The obvious alternative, and why it loses. You could just delete columns — keep mean radius, drop mean perimeter and mean area. That is feature selection, and everything you drop is gone. PCA instead combines: the new column keeps a share of all three. Nothing is thrown away; it is compressed.
🙈 This is unsupervised. PCA never sees a y. It cannot know which columns predict a malignant tumour, because nobody told it which tumours are malignant. It optimises one thing only: keeping the rows as spread out as it can. That works remarkably often — but it is spread, not usefulness, and panel 08 shows what happens when the two come apart.
DECK 18 · 02 / 10 · The idea
Variance
How spread out the numbers are — the only thing PCA optimises

Before any of the PCA machinery, one plain idea. Here are three columns of ten numbers each. Same average, different spread:

Nothing is hidden in that picture: the top row is bunched, the bottom row is scattered, and every row has its average in the same place. Variance is the number that tells those three rows apart. Now the formula — and it says exactly what you just looked at:

$$ s^2 \;=\; \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n} $$

Read it right to left: take each value's distance from the mean, square it so that being 4 below counts the same as 4 above, add them all up, and divide by how many there were. A big answer means scattered; zero means every value is identical.

Worked out, on the notebook's own example

The notebook uses X = [2, 4, 6, 8, 10]. The mean is \((2+4+6+8+10)/5 = 6\).

$$ s^2 = \frac{40}{5} = 8 \qquad\qquad s = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83 $$

The middle column summing to zero is not a coincidence — it is what "mean" means, and it is exactly why the squares are needed. Without them every variance in the world would be 0.

📏 Variance or standard deviation? The standard deviation \(s = \sqrt{s^2} \approx 2.83\) is the readable one — it is in the same units as the data, so "2.83 marks" means something. Variance is the one that adds up: the variance of several columns together is the sum of their variances, and that additivity is the whole reason PCA is stated in variance and not in standard deviation.
➗ Divide by n or by n − 1? Both appear, and the notebook uses both. Dividing by n is the variance of the numbers you have. Dividing by n − 1 estimates the variance of the wider population those numbers came from, and is what pandas' .var() and sklearn use by default. In the notebook's second example — ten people's weights, mean 70 kg, squared deviations adding to 229 — that gives \(229/9 \approx 25.44\), not \(229/10 = 22.9\). For 569 rows the difference is under a fifth of a percent; for 10 rows it is 11%.
DECK 18 · 03 / 10 · The idea
A component is a direction
PC1 is the arrow the data is longest along

A principal component is not a column of the table and not a row of it. It is a direction — an arrow through the cloud, written as one weight per original column. Below, every point is dropped straight down onto the candidate line, and the spread of those landing points is measured. Turn the line and watch the number move.

Find PC1 by hand
12°
the data projected onto the line each point's drop

The variance of those yellow points is highest at one particular angle, and that angle is PC1. Every other direction throws some of the spread away. PC2 is then fixed: it is the direction at 90° to PC1, mopping up what is left.

PC1

The direction of greatest variance in the data. Nothing is more spread out than this.

PC2

Orthogonal to PC1 — at a right angle — and the most spread out of everything that is left.

all of them

Every component is orthogonal to every other. They are a fresh set of axes for the same space.

length 1

Every component is normalised: its weights squared add up to 1. It is a direction, not a distance.

📐 Why orthogonal matters. If PC2 pointed anywhere near PC1 it would re-measure the same spread twice, and the percentages in panel 06 would double-count. Right angles make the components independent, which is what lets you add their explained variances up and get a meaningful total.
🔧 How sklearn actually finds it — and why you can skip it. It does not spin a line the way the demo does. It builds the covariance matrix and takes its eigenvectors, or equivalently runs an SVD; the top eigenvector is PC1 and its eigenvalue is the variance along it. That is a linear-algebra course. You do not need it to use PCA, because fit hands you the finished directions — and from there, as the next panel shows, it is one multiplication.
DECK 18 · 04 / 10 · Using it
Multiply · one number
Row times component, add it up, and the row has one coordinate

This is the panel that matters. Once fit has handed you a component, using PCA is arithmetic you could do on paper: multiply each of the row's values by the matching weight, add the products, and that single total is the row's new coordinate. It is called the projection of the row onto that component.

$$ \text{projection} \;=\; x_1 w_1 + x_2 w_2 + \dots + x_p w_p $$

One row in, one number out. Pick an example and step through it:

Row × component
projection —

The notebook's example, in full

One student's marks, and a component that weights maths hardest:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Obs}_1 &= [\,85,\; 90,\; 80\,] \quad\text{(maths, english, science)} \\ \text{PC}_1 &= [\,0.5,\; 0.3,\; 0.2\,] \\[4pt] \text{projection} &= 85 \times 0.5 \;+\; 90 \times 0.3 \;+\; 80 \times 0.2 \\ &= 42.5 + 27 + 16 \;=\; \mathbf{85.5} \end{aligned} $$

Three numbers went in. One number came out. That is the entire dimensionality reduction, for one row and one component — and the row is now a single point on a single axis rather than a point in a 3-D space.

[85, 90, 80] × [0.5, 0.3, 0.2] → 85.5
🔁 Want two columns instead of one? Do it twice. Multiply the same row by PC2 and you get a second number. n_components=2 means sklearn hands you two weight vectors, does both multiplications for every row, and returns a table with two columns. n_components=5 means five. The row never changes; only how many directions you measure it along.
🧾 What the number means. On scaled data, a projection is a signed distance along the component, measured from the middle of the cloud. In the tumour data, row 0 lands at 9.19 on PC1 while row 1 lands at 2.39 — so row 0 sits much further out along the "big, irregular nucleus" direction. The sign is arbitrary and can flip between library versions; the gaps between rows are what carry the information.
⚠️ The one condition. This multiplication only means what it should if the row was scaled by the same StandardScaler that was fitted before fit, and if the component's weights line up with the row's columns in the same order. Get either wrong and the arithmetic still runs and still returns a number — a meaningless one. This is why the whole thing belongs in a Pipeline (panel 09).
DECK 18 · 05 / 10 · Using it
30 columns → 2
The same multiplication, 569 rows at a time

Scale it up and nothing changes conceptually — it is the previous panel's multiplication, run for every row and every component. Here is the notebook's code, and the shape of every object it touches:

python
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

df = pd.read_csv('tumor_data_features.csv')   # (569, 30)

scaler      = StandardScaler()
scaled_data = scaler.fit_transform(df)              # (569, 30)  still 30 columns

pca        = PCA(n_components=2)
pca_result = pca.fit_transform(scaled_data)       # (569, 2)   <- two columns

print(pca.components_.shape)                      # (2, 30)
print(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
objectshapewhat one entry is
scaled_data(569, 30)one measurement of one tumour, as a z-score
pca.components_(2, 30)one weight — how much column j counts towards component i
pca_result(569, 2)one projection — row i multiplied by component j
explained_variance_ratio_(2,)the share of total spread that component keeps

components_ is the piece people misread. It is not the reduced data — it has 30 columns, not 569 rows. It is the recipe: two rows of thirty weights, and each one is the ingredient list for a new column.

🧮 Row 0, checked by hand. Take the first scaled tumour row and PC1's thirty weights, multiply them pairwise, add: the first five terms alone are 0.240, −0.215, 0.289, 0.218, 0.224, and all thirty add to 9.1928. That is exactly what pca_result[0, 0] contains. transform is np.dot in a coat — nothing more.
🏷️ The new columns have no names. PC1 is not "size" and PC2 is not "texture"; they are blends of all thirty measurements. You can look at components_[0] and see which weights are large to guess a meaning — on this data PC1 loads positively on almost everything, so it reads roughly as "how extreme is this nucleus overall" — but that is your interpretation, not something PCA gives you. This is the price of the compression.
📉 What was actually paid. Going from 30 columns to 2 dropped 36.8% of the spread on the floor. That is not free, and the next two panels are about deciding whether it was a good trade.
DECK 18 · 06 / 10 · Using it
Explained variance
What fraction of the spread each new column kept

Start with the notebook's two-variable example, because the arithmetic is small enough to see all of. Ten people, measured twice:

100
variance of height in cm² — a standard deviation of about 10 cm
25
variance of weight in kg² — a standard deviation of about 5 kg
125
total variance in the dataset — variances simply add

That 125 is the whole pot. Explained variance ratio is each component's slice of it:

$$ \text{explained variance ratio}_i \;=\; \frac{\text{variance along component } i}{\text{total variance}} $$

If a first component came out carrying 100 of the 125, it would have an explained variance ratio of \(100/125 = 0.80\) — it keeps 80% of everything the two original columns had between them, in one column instead of two.

The same sum on the real 30-column table

After scaling, every one of the 30 columns has variance 1, so the total variance is exactly 30. Running the notebook's fit:

📊 Why the bars only fall. PC1 takes the most spread available; PC2 takes the most of what is left, which is necessarily less; and so on down. explained_variance_ratio_ is always in descending order — you never have to sort it, and component 7 can never beat component 6.
🎯 "Explained" means spread, not truth. A high ratio says the component kept the geometry of the cloud. It does not say the component predicts anything. On this dataset it happens to do both — a scatter of PC1 against PC2 separates benign from malignant rather well — but that is a property of the data, not a guarantee from PCA.
DECK 18 · 07 / 10 · Using it
How many components?
Add them up until the curve stops climbing

Two components was a choice, not a law. The notebook makes the choice visible by fitting PCA thirty times — once for each possible number of components — and adding up the ratios each time:

python
explained = []
for i in range(1, 31):              # 30 original features
    pca = PCA(n_components=i)
    pca.fit(scaled_data)
    explained.append(sum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_))

plt.plot(range(1, 31), explained, marker='o')
plt.xlabel('Number of Principal Components')
plt.ylabel('Cumulative Explained Variance')
plt.grid(True)
plt.show()

Drag the cut and read off what you keep and what you pay for it:

The shape is the point. The first few components are enormously productive — component 1 alone buys 44%, and by component 6 you are at 89% with a fifth of the columns. After that each new component adds a percent or less, and the curve flattens into a wall.

2
components → 63.2%. The choice the notebook makes, because 2 is what you can plot.

10

17
components → 99.1%. Past here you are storing rounding error.
✂️ Two honest ways to cut, and one shortcut. Cut at a target — "whatever it takes to reach 95%". Or cut at the elbow, the same bend you looked for in the K-Means inertia curve, where extra components stop buying anything. The shortcut: PCA(n_components=0.95) — hand it a float below 1 and sklearn picks the count for you. On this data that returns 10.
📈 Compare this with the K-Means elbow. The inertia curve in deck 17 fell and you looked for a bend; this curve rises and you look for a plateau. Both are the same judgement in opposite directions: find where the return on one more unit of complexity collapses. Unlike the K-Means curve on the marketing data, this one has a genuinely sharp bend — the tumour columns really are that redundant.
🐢 That loop is wasteful, and it is fine. It fits PCA thirty times when one fit would do: the ratios are already in descending order, so np.cumsum(PCA().fit(scaled_data).explained_variance_ratio_) gives the identical curve from a single fit. On 569 rows nobody notices. On 500,000 rows the loop is the difference between a coffee break and a meeting.
DECK 18 · 08 / 10 · Making it work
Scale first
PCA maximises variance — so it will chase whichever column has the biggest units

K-Means needed scaling because it measures distance. PCA needs it for a sharper reason: variance is the thing it optimises, and variance is measured in the square of the column's units. Change a column from centimetres to millimetres and its variance goes up a hundredfold — without one atom of new information.

In the tumour table, worst area has a raw variance of about 323,600 and mean smoothness about 0.0002. Run PCA on that unscaled and the outcome is not in doubt:

❌ PCA(df) — no scaling

  • explained_variance_ratio_ = [0.982, 0.016]
  • PC1 "explains" 98.2% of everything
  • Its two big weights: worst area 0.852, mean area 0.517 — the other 28 columns are noise around zero
  • PC1 is not a discovery. It is the area column with extra steps.

✅ PCA(scaled_data)

  • explained_variance_ratio_ = [0.443, 0.190]
  • Every column arrives with variance 1 and gets an equal hearing
  • PC1's weights are spread across all 30 — the largest is 0.261
  • PC1 is a real summary: "how extreme is this nucleus overall"
🎭 The trap is that the bad one looks better. 98.2% from a single component is a lovely number to put in a slide, and it is completely empty — it says nothing except that areas are measured in big units. Whenever a first component explains almost everything, check the scaler before you celebrate.
⚖️ StandardScaler, not MinMaxScaler. StandardScaler makes every column's variance exactly 1, which is precisely the property PCA needs. MinMaxScaler squeezes every column into 0–1 but leaves their variances unequal — a column with one outlier ends up squashed into a tiny range with almost no variance left. For PCA, standardise.
🤔 The one time you skip it. If every column is already the same quantity in the same unit — pixel intensities in an image, monthly returns of several stocks — then the differences in variance are real information and scaling would erase them. Different quantities in different units: always scale.
DECK 18 · 09 / 10 · Making it work
The whole recipe
Scale, fit, transform, check — and then wrap it up

Four steps, always in this order:

scale

StandardScaler().fit_transform(df) — every column to variance 1.

fit

PCA(n_components=k).fit(...) — find the directions. This is where the work happens.

transform

Multiply every row by every component. One number per row per component.

check

Read explained_variance_ratio_. If the total is low, k is too small.

python · the plain version
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
import numpy as np

scaler = StandardScaler()
Xs     = scaler.fit_transform(df)

pca = PCA(n_components=2)
Z   = pca.fit_transform(Xs)                 # (569, 2)

print(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)        # [0.4427 0.1897]
print(pca.explained_variance_ratio_.sum())  # 0.6324

# the two new columns, ready to plot or to feed a model
plt.scatter(Z[:, 0], Z[:, 1], s=12)
plt.xlabel('PC1'); plt.ylabel('PC2')

That is correct for exploring. The moment a model is involved, it is a bug waiting to happen — fit_transform on the whole table has already let the test rows influence the scaler and the components. The fix is the one from deck 16:

python · the version that goes in a model
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

pipe = Pipeline([
    ('scaler', StandardScaler()),
    ('pca',    PCA(n_components=0.95)),   # keep 95% -> picks 10 here
    ('clf',    LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
])

print(cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5).mean())

# and to see what PCA chose inside the fitted pipeline:
pipe.fit(X, y)
print(pipe.named_steps['pca'].n_components_)
🔒 Why the Pipeline is not optional here. PCA learns from the data — the components are fitted, exactly like the scaler's means. Fit them on all the rows and every cross-validation fold has already seen its own test set through the components, which quietly inflates the score. Inside a Pipeline, cross_val_score re-fits the scaler and the PCA on each training fold, which is the honest version.
🧭 Where PCA sits in the order. Encode categoricals first (PCA needs numbers), then scale, then PCA, then the model. Never PCA before scaling — that is panel 08 — and never PCA on the target column.

APPENDIX A1 · Reference
The PCA object
What you pass in, and what comes back with an underscore

Everything ending in an underscore was learned during fit and does not exist before it.

Arguments

n_components=k

An int keeps exactly k components. A float between 0 and 1 keeps as many as it takes to reach that much explained variance — 0.95 gives 10 on the tumour data. Leave it out and you keep all of them, which is the right move when you only want to look at the curve.

random_state=42

Only matters for svd_solver='randomized', which sklearn picks by itself on large tables. Set it anyway; it costs nothing and makes your run repeatable.

svd_solver='auto'

How the components get computed. 'auto' is right almost always. Worth knowing only because the randomized solver is what makes PCA fast on very wide data.

What fit leaves behind

components_

Shape (k, p) — k components, each with one weight per original column. Row 0 is PC1. This is the object you multiply a row by.

explained_variance_ratio_

Shape (k,), descending, summing to 1 if you kept everything. Each entry is that component's share of the total spread.

explained_variance_

The same thing before dividing — the actual variance along each component. On the scaled tumour data: 13.305 and 5.701. Divide by the total (30.05) and you are back to the ratios.

mean_

The column means subtracted before projecting. PCA always centres, even if you did not scale — which is why transform is really np.dot(X - mean_, components_.T).

n_components_

How many were actually kept. Worth printing when you passed a float, since that is the only way to find out what 0.95 turned into.

Methods

fit(X)

Finds the components. Returns nothing you want — the results are the attributes above.

transform(X)

The multiplication. (n, p) in, (n, k) out. Use it on new rows with a PCA already fitted on the training rows.

fit_transform(X)

Both at once, and slightly faster than doing them separately. Correct for the training set only.

inverse_transform(Z)

Multiplies back up: (n, k) in, (n, p) out. You do not get the original table back — you get its shadow, with everything the dropped components carried missing. Subtract the two and you are looking at exactly what PCA threw away.

🔍 Reading components_ like a human. pd.DataFrame(pca.components_, columns=df.columns) turns the weight matrix into a table you can actually sort. Large positive and large negative weights are both important; weights near zero mean that column barely takes part in this component.
APPENDIX A2 · Reference
PCA vs the neighbours
Three things it gets confused with, and the line between them

PCA and K-Means are both unsupervised and both often follow a StandardScaler, which is where the confusion starts. They do opposite things to the table.

PCAK-Means
works onthe columnsthe rows
gives youa narrower table, same number of rowsone group number per row, same columns
the number you choosen_componentsn_clusters
how to choose itcumulative explained variancethe elbow on inertia
what it optimisesvariance kept — maximisedistance to centroid — minimise
is it random?no — same answer every runyes — hence n_init
needs scalingyesyes

They are also natural partners: PCA down to 2 components, K-Means on the result, and now you can plot the clusters and actually see them. Cluster on the components rather than the raw table and the distances stop being dominated by whichever measurements happened to repeat.

PCA vs feature selection

SelectKBest keeps 10 of your 30 columns and deletes the rest — the survivors keep their names and their meaning. PCA keeps a bit of all 30 in each new column, loses the names, and keeps more of the information. Need to explain the model to someone? Select. Need the geometry? PCA.

PCA vs LDA

LDA does the same kind of projection but is supervised — it uses y, and looks for the direction that separates the classes rather than the direction with the most spread. If you have labels and reduction is for a classifier, LDA usually wins.

PCA vs t-SNE / UMAP

Those are for pictures only. They bend space to make clusters look tidy in 2-D, cannot transform new rows, and have no explained-variance number. PCA is linear, reversible and honest — and unlike them it can feed a model.

PCA is not a model

It has no predict and no score. It is a transformer, like StandardScaler: something you put before a model in a pipeline, never something you evaluate on its own.

🗜️ And it is a compressor. Deck 17 shrank an image by clustering its colours; PCA shrinks by dropping components — keep 50 of 500 columns, inverse_transform back, and you have a lossy reconstruction. The tumour table went from 569×30 to 569×2 plus a 2×30 recipe: 17,070 numbers down to 1,198, and 63% of the structure survives.
